26. India's 'JAM Trinity' stands for:

  1. Jan Dhan (bank accounts) + Aadhaar (unique identity) + Mobile — infrastructure for Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) — eliminating middlemen in welfare delivery
  2. Just Another Mission
  3. Joint Agricultural Market
  4. Judiciary-Administration-Military

Answer: Jan Dhan (bank accounts) + Aadhaar (unique identity) + Mobile — infrastructure for Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) — eliminating middlemen in welfare delivery

Explanation: JAM Trinity: proposed by Economic Survey 2014-15 (Arvind Subramanian, Chief Economic Adviser). Jan Dhan: bank accounts for all. Aadhaar: biometric unique ID. Mobile: connectivity and digital payments. JAM enables direct transfer of subsidies (LPG, fertilizer, MGNREGA wages, scholarships) to beneficiaries' accounts — reducing leakage and corruption.

27. The economic concept 'invisible hand' was given by:

  1. Adam Smith (in 'Wealth of Nations,' 1776) — the idea that individuals pursuing self-interest in free markets unintentionally promote public good through competition
  2. John Maynard Keynes
  3. Karl Marx
  4. Milton Friedman

Answer: Adam Smith (in 'Wealth of Nations,' 1776) — the idea that individuals pursuing self-interest in free markets unintentionally promote public good through competition

Explanation: Adam Smith's 'invisible hand': when individuals act in their own self-interest (profit motive) in competitive markets, they are guided as if by an 'invisible hand' to produce goods/services society wants at efficient prices. Basis of free-market capitalism. Wealth of Nations (1776) also argued for division of labor, free trade, and against mercantilism.

28. India's Scheduled Commercial Banks (SCBs) are categorized as:

  1. Public Sector Banks (27, including SBI), Private Sector Banks (21, e.g., HDFC, ICICI), Foreign Banks (46), Small Finance Banks (12), Payments Banks (6)
  2. Only public banks
  3. Only private banks
  4. Only cooperative banks

Answer: Public Sector Banks (27, including SBI), Private Sector Banks (21, e.g., HDFC, ICICI), Foreign Banks (46), Small Finance Banks (12), Payments Banks (6)

Explanation: India's banking structure: Scheduled Commercial Banks (listed in 2nd Schedule of RBI Act): Public Sector Banks (12, after consolidation — e.g., SBI, PNB, Bank of Baroda), Private Banks (21, e.g., HDFC, ICICI, Axis), Foreign Banks, Small Finance Banks, Payments Banks. Cooperative Banks: separate regulatory framework.

29. The Export Credit Guarantee Corporation (ECGC) of India provides:

  1. Credit insurance to Indian exporters against risk of non-payment by foreign buyers, and guarantees to banks that finance exports — established 1957, HQ Mumbai
  2. Direct export finance
  3. Import finance
  4. Port services

Answer: Credit insurance to Indian exporters against risk of non-payment by foreign buyers, and guarantees to banks that finance exports — established 1957, HQ Mumbai

Explanation: ECGC (Export Credit Guarantee Corporation, 1957, Ministry of Commerce): provides export credit insurance to protect Indian exporters against payment default by foreign buyers (due to commercial/political risk). Also gives counter-guarantees/guarantees to banks providing export finance. Enables Indian companies to export to risky markets with insurance protection.

30. Non-Performing Assets (NPAs) are loans where:

  1. Borrower has not made interest or principal repayment for more than 90 days — classified as Substandard (< 1 year), Doubtful (1-3 years), Loss (>3 years)
  2. Borrower is making regular payments
  3. Bank has invested in unproductive assets
  4. Government loans not repaid

Answer: Borrower has not made interest or principal repayment for more than 90 days — classified as Substandard (< 1 year), Doubtful (1-3 years), Loss (>3 years)

Explanation: NPA: loan where interest/principal is overdue for 90+ days. Classification: Substandard (NPA for <1 year), Doubtful (NPA for 1-3 years, collateral insufficient), Loss (uncollectable, to be written off). High NPAs reduce banks' profitability and ability to lend. India's NPA problem peaked ~2018 (GNPAs of ~11.2% of advances). IBC 2016 helped resolve many NPAs.

31. SARFAESI Act 2002 enables:

  1. Banks/NBFCs to recover non-performing assets by taking possession of secured assets without going to court — sell assets to recover dues; speeds up NPA recovery
  2. Only court-based NPA recovery
  3. Transfer of NPA to government
  4. Waiver of NPA by RBI

Answer: Banks/NBFCs to recover non-performing assets by taking possession of secured assets without going to court — sell assets to recover dues; speeds up NPA recovery

Explanation: SARFAESI (Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002): banks can: (1) issue demand notice to defaulter, (2) take symbolic/physical possession of collateral after 60 days without court intervention, (3) sell/manage asset or assign to ARC (Asset Reconstruction Company). Major tool for NPA recovery along with DRT (Debt Recovery Tribunal).

32. Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) were established under:

  1. RRB Act 1976 (Narashimham Committee recommendation) — jointly owned by Central Government (50%), State Government (15%), and Sponsor Commercial Bank (35%) — serve rural credit needs
  2. NABARD Act
  3. Banking Regulation Act 1949
  4. RBI Act 1934

Answer: RRB Act 1976 (Narashimham Committee recommendation) — jointly owned by Central Government (50%), State Government (15%), and Sponsor Commercial Bank (35%) — serve rural credit needs

Explanation: RRBs (1975/1976): established to provide banking services in rural areas (disadvantaged by cooperative banks' limitations). First RRB: Prathama Grameen Bank (Moradabad, 1975). Ownership: Centre 50%, State 15%, Sponsor Bank 35%. RBI Governor's advice: each district should have an RRB. Now consolidated: 43 RRBs (from 196 originally). Regulated by NABARD.

33. The 'Small Finance Banks' are meant to serve:

  1. Small business units, small/marginal farmers, micro/small industries, and unorganized sector entities — RBI licensed them in 2014-15; must maintain 75% of loans to priority sector
  2. Only savings deposits
  3. Only large corporations
  4. Only agricultural sector

Answer: Small business units, small/marginal farmers, micro/small industries, and unorganized sector entities — RBI licensed them in 2014-15; must maintain 75% of loans to priority sector

Explanation: Small Finance Banks (SFBs): licensed by RBI (guidelines 2014, first banks 2015-16). Capital: Rs 200 crore minimum. Functions: basic banking (deposits, loans). Mandate: 75% of loans must go to priority sector (vs 40% for SCBs). Loan size: up to Rs 25 lakh to max Rs 2 crore. Examples: AU Small Finance Bank, Equitas, Ujjivan, Jana Bank. Many converted from MFIs.

34. Payments Banks differ from regular banks in that they:

  1. Can accept deposits (up to Rs 2 lakh per customer) but CANNOT give loans or issue credit cards — primarily for remittances and mobile banking; 6 operational as of 2023
  2. Can only give loans, not deposits
  3. Are only for international transactions
  4. Are only for corporate clients

Answer: Can accept deposits (up to Rs 2 lakh per customer) but CANNOT give loans or issue credit cards — primarily for remittances and mobile banking; 6 operational as of 2023

Explanation: Payments Banks (RBI guidelines 2014): can accept deposits (up to Rs 2 lakh per account), issue debit cards, facilitate remittances, and offer internet banking. CANNOT: issue credit cards, make loans, accept NRI deposits. Examples: Paytm Payments Bank, Airtel Payments Bank, India Post Payments Bank, Jio Payments Bank. 75% of deposits must be in government securities.

35. The Banking Ombudsman Scheme provides:

  1. Free, independent complaint resolution for banking customers against deficient services — operated by RBI; now replaced by Reserve Bank – Integrated Ombudsman Scheme (RB-IOS) 2021 covering banks, NBFCs, and payment systems
  2. Legal action against banks
  3. Compensation by government
  4. Only for corporate customers

Answer: Free, independent complaint resolution for banking customers against deficient services — operated by RBI; now replaced by Reserve Bank – Integrated Ombudsman Scheme (RB-IOS) 2021 covering banks, NBFCs, and payment systems

Explanation: Banking Ombudsman (BO): customer can file complaint if bank doesn't resolve within 30 days. BO can award compensation up to Rs 20 lakh. RB-IOS 2021: unified scheme covering commercial banks, cooperative banks, NBFCs, and non-bank PPIs (payment instruments). Centralized receipt: complaints filed online at cms.rbi.org.in. Turnaround: 30 days resolution target.

36. The Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) is:

  1. Multilateral development bank proposed by China (2013), established 2015 — 106 members; HQ Beijing; funds infrastructure in Asia; India is the 2nd largest shareholder
  2. ADB branch
  3. World Bank subsidiary
  4. IMF initiative

Answer: Multilateral development bank proposed by China (2013), established 2015 — 106 members; HQ Beijing; funds infrastructure in Asia; India is the 2nd largest shareholder

Explanation: AIIB (Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, 2015): proposed by China's Xi Jinping. 57 founding members. HQ: Beijing. Focus: infrastructure (energy, transport, urban, rural, communications) in Asia. China: largest shareholder (~26%); India: 2nd largest (~8%). The US and Japan did not join (seeing it as China's geopolitical tool). India has received several AIIB loans for transport and urban infrastructure.

37. Open Market Operations (OMO) by RBI involve:

  1. RBI buying or selling government securities in the open market to inject or withdraw liquidity — buying injects money into banking system (increases money supply), selling withdraws
  2. Only foreign currency operations
  3. Only lending to banks
  4. Only regulatory actions

Answer: RBI buying or selling government securities in the open market to inject or withdraw liquidity — buying injects money into banking system (increases money supply), selling withdraws

Explanation: OMO: primary tool of RBI's liquidity management. OMO purchase (bond purchase): RBI buys G-Secs from banks → banks get cash → more liquidity in system → lower interest rates. OMO sale: RBI sells G-Secs → banks give cash to RBI → less liquidity → higher rates. Twist OMOs: buy long-term, sell short-term bonds to control yield curve.

38. The MUDRA (Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency) Bank refinances:

  1. MFIs (Microfinance Institutions), NBFCs, commercial banks, and cooperative banks that provide loans to micro/small enterprises — does not directly lend to end borrowers
  2. Directly to street vendors only
  3. Large corporations
  4. Export companies

Answer: MFIs (Microfinance Institutions), NBFCs, commercial banks, and cooperative banks that provide loans to micro/small enterprises — does not directly lend to end borrowers

Explanation: MUDRA Bank (2015, subsidiary of SIDBI): does not lend directly to end borrowers. Refinances intermediaries (MFIs, NBFCs, SFBs, RRBs, commercial banks, cooperative banks) that lend to micro and small enterprises. MUDRA also provides credit guarantees. Rs 3 lakh crore disbursed under PM Mudra Yojana since 2015.

39. Financial inclusion in India is measured by:

  1. Access to formal financial services (bank account, insurance, credit, pension) — India has achieved 'bank account for all' via PMJDY but usage (active accounts) remains a challenge
  2. Only number of bank branches
  3. ATM density
  4. Only credit card holders

Answer: Access to formal financial services (bank account, insurance, credit, pension) — India has achieved 'bank account for all' via PMJDY but usage (active accounts) remains a challenge

Explanation: Financial inclusion: ensuring all people have access to affordable financial products — bank accounts, credit, insurance, remittances. India's progress: PMJDY opened 52+ crore accounts; FI Index (RBI, 2021): 53.9 (out of 100). Challenges: dormant accounts, last-mile access, digital literacy. Business Correspondents (BCs): banking agents in unbanked areas.

40. Gold Monetization Scheme (GMS) was launched to:

  1. Mobilize idle gold held by households and trusts (estimated 25,000+ tonnes); depositors earn interest; gold used for banks' lending or import substitution — launched 2015
  2. Nationalize gold
  3. Reduce gold imports via tax
  4. Set gold prices

Answer: Mobilize idle gold held by households and trusts (estimated 25,000+ tonnes); depositors earn interest; gold used for banks' lending or import substitution — launched 2015

Explanation: Gold Monetization Scheme (2015, replaced GDS 1999): households and institutions deposit physical gold (minimum 10 grams) with banks. Earn interest (2.25-2.50% p.a.) + get back gold or equivalent value. Banks can lend to jewellers or sell. Aims to reduce India's gold imports (~800-900 tonnes/year) by mobilizing India's estimated 25,000 tonnes of privately held gold.

41. Priority Sector Lending (PSL) norms require banks to lend:

  1. 40% of Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) to priority sectors — agriculture (18%), MSMEs, education, housing, renewable energy, social infrastructure, weaker sections
  2. 50% to agriculture only
  3. No specific requirement
  4. Only to government projects

Answer: 40% of Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) to priority sectors — agriculture (18%), MSMEs, education, housing, renewable energy, social infrastructure, weaker sections

Explanation: PSL: RBI mandates Scheduled Commercial Banks lend 40% of ANBC to priority sectors. Subsectors: Agriculture (18% of ANBC, of which 8% for small/marginal farmers), MSMEs, Education, Housing (affordable), Renewable Energy, Social Infrastructure. Foreign banks with <20 branches: 40% (different sub-targets). Priority Sector Lending Certificates (PSLCs) allow trading PSL shortfalls/surplus among banks.

42. The Kisan Credit Card (KCC) scheme was launched to:

  1. Provide timely and adequate credit to farmers for agricultural needs (seeds, fertilizers, pesticides, post-harvest) at subsidized interest rates (7% with 3% interest subvention for prompt repayment)
  2. Only for tractor purchase
  3. Personal loans for farmers
  4. Export finance for farmers

Answer: Provide timely and adequate credit to farmers for agricultural needs (seeds, fertilizers, pesticides, post-harvest) at subsidized interest rates (7% with 3% interest subvention for prompt repayment)

Explanation: KCC (1998, based on RV Gupta Committee): short-term revolving credit facility for: crop cultivation expenses, post-harvest expenses, farm maintenance, allied activities, consumption needs. Interest: 7% p.a. (government subvention of 3% for prompt payment → effective 4%). Credit limit: crop loan + 10% for post-harvest + 20% for farm maintenance + 10% insurance. Now being made digital (Kisan Credit Card via mobile app).

43. Scheduled Banks in India are those listed in:

  1. Second Schedule of the RBI Act 1934 — these banks satisfy RBI's eligibility criteria and get RBI benefits (e.g., refinance facility, clearing membership); most commercial banks are scheduled
  2. First Schedule
  3. Third Schedule
  4. Banking Regulation Act schedule

Answer: Second Schedule of the RBI Act 1934 — these banks satisfy RBI's eligibility criteria and get RBI benefits (e.g., refinance facility, clearing membership); most commercial banks are scheduled

Explanation: Scheduled Banks: listed in 2nd Schedule of RBI Act 1934. To be listed: paid-up capital + reserves ≥ Rs 5 lakh (historical, now effectively much higher). Benefits: can borrow from RBI, become member of clearinghouse. Non-scheduled banks: some cooperative banks. All public/private/foreign/SFBs/payment banks are scheduled.

44. The term 'Repo Rate' versus 'Reverse Repo Rate' can be understood as:

  1. Repo: rate at which banks borrow from RBI (selling securities); Reverse Repo: rate at which banks park excess funds with RBI (RBI borrows from banks) — Reverse Repo is lower than Repo
  2. Repo is lower than Reverse Repo
  3. They are the same
  4. Only one of these is used by RBI

Answer: Repo: rate at which banks borrow from RBI (selling securities); Reverse Repo: rate at which banks park excess funds with RBI (RBI borrows from banks) — Reverse Repo is lower than Repo

Explanation: Repo Rate: banks need short-term funds → sell G-Secs to RBI with agreement to repurchase → RBI charges repo rate. Reverse Repo: banks have excess funds → park with RBI (RBI issues securities or gives written promise) → RBI pays reverse repo rate. Reverse Repo < Repo (currently Repo - 0.25%). Corridor: Reverse Repo (floor) to MSF (Marginal Standing Facility, Repo + 0.25%, ceiling).

45. The National Mission for Financial Inclusion (NMFI) is also known as:

  1. Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) — launched August 28, 2014; world's largest financial inclusion drive; zero-balance accounts, RuPay cards, insurance, overdraft facility
  2. Mudra Yojana
  3. Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana
  4. Atal Pension Yojana

Answer: Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) — launched August 28, 2014; world's largest financial inclusion drive; zero-balance accounts, RuPay cards, insurance, overdraft facility

Explanation: PMJDY (launched 28 Aug 2014 — 'Financial Inclusion Day'): National Mission for Financial Inclusion. Targets: every household gets one bank account, all adults get RuPay debit card (with Rs 1-2 lakh accident insurance), overdraft facility of Rs 10,000. Achieved: 52+ crore accounts, 68% active, Rs 2 lakh crore+ deposits. Guinness record for most accounts opened in 1 week.

46. The National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM/Aajeevika) focuses on:

  1. Organizing rural poor into Self-Help Groups (SHGs) — credit linking, livelihood promotion, skill training; targets 10 crore rural poor women in 600 districts; launched 2011
  2. Only individual farmers
  3. Only agricultural credit
  4. Only employment guarantee (MGNREGA does that)

Answer: Organizing rural poor into Self-Help Groups (SHGs) — credit linking, livelihood promotion, skill training; targets 10 crore rural poor women in 600 districts; launched 2011

Explanation: NRLM (2011, renamed Aajeevika, now renamed DAY-NRLM — Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana): world's largest poverty alleviation program. Promotes SHGs (12-20 women) → federations (village organization) → cluster level federations → block/district level federations. Provides: bank credit linkage (SHG-BLP), livelihood promotion, skill training, financial literacy.

  1. ~Rs 1.97 lakh per year (approximately $2,400 at market exchange rate) — making India a lower-middle income country by World Bank classification
  2. Rs 50,000 per year
  3. Rs 10 lakh per year
  4. Rs 5 lakh per year

Answer: ~Rs 1.97 lakh per year (approximately $2,400 at market exchange rate) — making India a lower-middle income country by World Bank classification

Explanation: India's per capita NNP (National Income per person) at current prices: ~Rs 1.97 lakh (2022-23) ≈ $2,400 at market exchange rate. At PPP: ~$8,000. World Bank classification (2022-23): Lower-Middle Income ($1,086-$4,255 per capita GNI). India crossed the $2,000 per capita mark. Contrast: Norway/USA ~$65,000+ per capita. Wide intra-India variation (Goa ~$5,500 vs Bihar ~$700).

48. India's MUDRA loan scheme has three categories named after:

  1. Three stages of business growth: Shishu (baby — up to Rs 50,000 for new/small businesses), Kishore (adolescent — Rs 50,000-5 lakh, for established micro units), Tarun (young adult — Rs 5-10 lakh, for medium micro units)
  2. Three rivers
  3. Three goddesses
  4. Three states of India

Tarun Plus (2024): up to Rs 20 lakh for Tarun category with good track record. All collateral-free. Interest rates typically 10-12%.

49. The term 'repo' stands for:

  1. Repurchase Agreement — short-term borrowing: seller agrees to sell securities today and repurchase them at a higher price in future; difference in price is the interest (repo rate)
  2. Repayment Order
  3. Republic Policy Order
  4. Regular Policy Operation

Answer: Repurchase Agreement — short-term borrowing: seller agrees to sell securities today and repurchase them at a higher price in future; difference in price is the interest (repo rate)

Explanation: Repo (Repurchase Agreement): one party sells securities (government bonds) to another and agrees to repurchase them at a later date at a specified (higher) price. The difference represents the interest (repo rate). In RBI context: commercial banks sell G-Secs to RBI for overnight liquidity, agreeing to repurchase next day. Reverse Repo: opposite direction — banks buy from RBI.

  1. 12 PSBs (after multiple mergers: 2019 — Vijaya Bank + Dena Bank merged into Bank of Baroda; 2020 — 10 PSBs merged into 4) — plus SBI which merged 5 associates in 2017

  4. 5 PSBs

Answer: 12 PSBs (after multiple mergers: 2019 — Vijaya Bank + Dena Bank merged into Bank of Baroda; 2020 — 10 PSBs merged into 4) — plus SBI which merged 5 associates in 2017

April 2020 (largest consolidation): Oriental Bank of Commerce + United Bank → PNB; Syndicate Bank → Canara Bank; Andhra Bank + Corporation Bank → Union Bank; Allahabad Bank → Indian Bank.
