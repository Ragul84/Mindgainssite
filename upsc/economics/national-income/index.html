National Income MCQs with answers

Use these 25 worked National Income questions to revise the UPSC Economy syllabus. Each answer includes an explanation; the full practice set contains 654 questions.

1. GDP stands for:

  1. Gross Domestic Product
  2. General Domestic Price
  3. Gross Development Plan
  4. Government Domestic Policy

Answer: Gross Domestic Product

Explanation: GDP (Gross Domestic Product) is the total monetary value of all goods and services produced within a country's borders in a specific period.

2. The 'Human Development Index' (HDI) is published by:

  1. World Bank
  2. IMF
  3. UNDP (United Nations Development Programme)
  4. WHO

Answer: UNDP (United Nations Development Programme)

Explanation: The HDI is published by UNDP in the Human Development Report. It measures countries on three dimensions: life expectancy, education, and per capita income.

3. The term 'stagflation' refers to:

  1. High growth with low inflation
  2. Stagnation (low growth/unemployment) combined with high inflation
  3. Deflation with high growth
  4. Very rapid economic growth

Answer: Stagnation (low growth/unemployment) combined with high inflation

Explanation: Stagflation is the combination of high inflation AND economic stagnation (slow growth + high unemployment) — a particularly difficult economic condition, as standard remedies worsen one problem while solving the other.

4. Gross Domestic Product (GDP) measures:

  1. Total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a specific time period (usually a year/quarter)
  2. Total income of all citizens including abroad
  3. Only industrial production
  4. Only exports

Answer: Total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a specific time period (usually a year/quarter)

Explanation: GDP = C + I + G + (X-M) [Expenditure method]. C = Private Consumption, I = Investment, G = Government Spending, X = Exports, M = Imports. GDP measures economic output within borders regardless of who produces it. India's GDP at current prices: ~$3.7 trillion (2024). Nominal GDP (current prices) vs Real GDP (constant prices, adjusts for inflation).

5. The difference between GDP and GNP is:

  1. GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA) — GNP includes income of nationals abroad; GDP includes income of foreigners in India
  2. GDP is larger than GNP always
  3. They are the same thing
  4. GNP excludes government spending

Answer: GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA) — GNP includes income of nationals abroad; GDP includes income of foreigners in India

Explanation: GDP: value of production within the territory. GNP: value of production by nationals (regardless of where). GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA). For India: NFIA is slightly positive (remittances from Indian workers abroad exceed MNC profits repatriated out). GNP ≈ GNI (Gross National Income).

6. Net National Product (NNP) at factor cost is also known as:

  1. National Income — NNP at factor cost = GNP - Depreciation (capital consumption) — measures actual income generated by an economy
  2. Per capita income
  3. GDP
  4. Fiscal deficit

Answer: National Income — NNP at factor cost = GNP - Depreciation (capital consumption) — measures actual income generated by an economy

Explanation: National Income = NNP at factor cost = GNP - Depreciation. Depreciation/capital consumption allowance: value of fixed assets used up in production. At factor cost = at market price minus indirect taxes plus subsidies. National Income per capita = National Income ÷ Population = measure of average living standard.

7. The Second Five Year Plan (1956-61) was based on the model by:

  1. P.C. Mahalanobis — emphasized heavy industrialization (steel plants: Bhilai, Durgapur, Rourkela) and public sector growth
  2. Mahatma Gandhi
  3. Jawaharlal Nehru alone
  4. IMF recommendation

Answer: P.C. Mahalanobis — emphasized heavy industrialization (steel plants: Bhilai, Durgapur, Rourkela) and public sector growth

Explanation: Second FYP (Mahalanobis Plan, 1956-61): focused on heavy industry and capital goods (Feldman-Mahalanobis model — investment in capital goods to boost long-term growth). Steel plants: Bhilai (Soviet assistance), Durgapur (British), Rourkela (West German). Also established IITs and other institutions.

8. India adopted economic liberalization in:

  1. 1991 — LPG (Liberalization, Privatization, Globalization) reforms under PM Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Dr. Manmohan Singh during a severe balance of payments crisis
  2. 1947
  3. 1969 (bank nationalization)
  4. 2000

Answer: 1991 — LPG (Liberalization, Privatization, Globalization) reforms under PM Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Dr. Manmohan Singh during a severe balance of payments crisis

Explanation: 1991 Economic Reforms: India faced severe forex crisis (only 3 weeks' reserves), high inflation, and fiscal deficit. PM P.V. Narasimha Rao and FM Dr. Manmohan Singh introduced: dismantling License Raj, reducing import tariffs, opening FDI, devaluing rupee, privatization. Called LPG reforms. India's GDP growth accelerated post-reform.

9. The term 'fiscal deficit' refers to:

  1. Excess of total government expenditure over total revenue receipts (tax + non-tax revenue) — financed by borrowing
  2. Excess of exports over imports (trade surplus)
  3. Excess of imports over exports (trade deficit)
  4. Excess of bank deposits over loans

Answer: Excess of total government expenditure over total revenue receipts (tax + non-tax revenue) — financed by borrowing

Explanation: Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure - (Revenue Receipts + Capital Receipts excluding borrowings). It shows government's borrowing requirement. India's fiscal deficit target: 3% of GDP (FRBM Act, 2003 target). Funded by borrowing from RBI (monetization, inflationary) or market borrowings (public debt). High fiscal deficit → high government debt.

10. The FRBM Act (Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act) was passed in:

  1. 2003 — to reduce fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP and eliminate revenue deficit; mandated fiscal consolidation
  2. 1991
  3. 2000
  4. 2010

Answer: 2003 — to reduce fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP and eliminate revenue deficit; mandated fiscal consolidation

Explanation: FRBM Act, 2003: compels government to reduce fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP and eliminate revenue deficit. Requires medium-term fiscal policy statement and macroeconomic framework with each Union Budget. Escape clause: allows deviation during natural calamities, national security threats, or severe recessions.

11. The Human Development Index (HDI) was developed by:

  1. UNDP (United Nations Development Programme) — Mahbub ul Haq (Pakistan) and Amartya Sen (India); measures health, education, and income (not just GDP)
  2. World Bank
  3. IMF
  4. WHO

Answer: UNDP (United Nations Development Programme) — Mahbub ul Haq (Pakistan) and Amartya Sen (India); measures health, education, and income (not just GDP)

Explanation: HDI (1990): developed by economist Mahbub ul Haq and Amartya Sen for UNDP. Composite index measuring: Life Expectancy (health), Mean/Expected Years of Schooling (education), and GNI per capita (income). HDI ranges 0-1. India's HDI rank: 134 out of 193 (UNDP 2023 report). Norway consistently tops HDI.

12. SIDBI (Small Industries Development Bank of India) was established to:

  1. Finance and develop Micro, Small & Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) — provides refinancing and direct loans; established 1990, HQ: Lucknow
  2. Finance agriculture
  3. Fund large industries
  4. Manage stock markets

Answer: Finance and develop Micro, Small & Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) — provides refinancing and direct loans; established 1990, HQ: Lucknow

Explanation: SIDBI (1990, HQ Lucknow): principal financial institution for MSME promotion, financing, and development. Functions: refinancing commercial banks/SFCs/NBFCs for MSME loans, direct lending, equity assistance, microfinance support. MSMEs contribute ~30% of India's GDP and ~50% of exports.

13. India's economic growth rate (GDP growth) is measured by:

  1. Central Statistics Office (CSO) — now called NSO (National Statistical Office) under MoSPI; base year currently 2011-12
  2. RBI
  3. Finance Ministry directly
  4. World Bank

Answer: Central Statistics Office (CSO) — now called NSO (National Statistical Office) under MoSPI; base year currently 2011-12

Explanation: National Statistical Office (NSO, formerly CSO) under Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) compiles India's national accounts (GDP data). Current base year: 2011-12. GDP growth rate: ~7% in recent years (one of fastest growing major economies). IMF, World Bank provide their own GDP estimates.

14. The 'trickle-down' theory of economic growth argues:

  1. Benefits of economic growth in the top/corporate sector eventually 'trickle down' to poorer sections through employment, investment, and tax revenues — criticized as insufficient for poverty reduction
  2. Wealth redistributed directly
  3. Growth benefits everyone equally
  4. Only the poor should be targeted

Answer: Benefits of economic growth in the top/corporate sector eventually 'trickle down' to poorer sections through employment, investment, and tax revenues — criticized as insufficient for poverty reduction

Explanation: Trickle-down economics (supply-side economics): tax cuts and benefits to rich/corporations will create investment, jobs, and economic activity that benefits all. Critics (e.g., Amartya Sen, Joseph Stiglitz): growth doesn't automatically reduce inequality; direct investment in human capital (health, education) is more effective for inclusive growth.

15. The concept of 'micro, small, and medium enterprises' (MSMEs) in India is defined based on:

  1. Investment in plant & machinery/equipment AND annual turnover — revised in 2020: Micro (up to Rs 1 cr investment, Rs 5 cr turnover), Small (up to Rs 10 cr, Rs 50 cr), Medium (up to Rs 50 cr, Rs 250 cr)
  2. Number of employees only
  3. Capital employed only
  4. Area of operation

Answer: Investment in plant & machinery/equipment AND annual turnover — revised in 2020: Micro (up to Rs 1 cr investment, Rs 5 cr turnover), Small (up to Rs 10 cr, Rs 50 cr), Medium (up to Rs 50 cr, Rs 250 cr)

Explanation: Revised MSME definition (May 2020): Micro: investment ≤Rs 1 crore AND turnover ≤Rs 5 crore. Small: investment ≤Rs 10 crore AND turnover ≤Rs 50 crore. Medium: investment ≤Rs 50 crore AND turnover ≤Rs 250 crore. MSMEs: ~63 million units, 30% of GDP, 45% of exports, 110+ million employed.

16. India's largest source of tax revenue to the central government is:

  1. Goods and Services Tax (GST) / indirect taxes (combined) — and within direct taxes, Corporate Income Tax exceeds Personal Income Tax
  2. Wealth tax
  3. Customs duty
  4. Agricultural income tax

Answer: Goods and Services Tax (GST) / indirect taxes (combined) — and within direct taxes, Corporate Income Tax exceeds Personal Income Tax

Explanation: India's tax revenue structure: Total taxes: GST (largest indirect tax) + Income tax (direct tax). Within direct taxes: corporate tax ~53%, personal income tax ~47%. GST revenues: ~Rs 1.6 lakh crore/month (2023-24). Customs duty: on imports. India's tax-GDP ratio: ~11%, lower than OECD average (~34%).

17. Long-run economic growth is determined by:

  1. Improvements in productivity (technology, education, health, institutions) and accumulation of physical capital (Solow growth model)
  2. Short-term demand fluctuations
  3. Monetary policy alone
  4. Exchange rate movements

Answer: Improvements in productivity (technology, education, health, institutions) and accumulation of physical capital (Solow growth model)

Explanation: Long-run growth determinants (Robert Solow's model): capital accumulation (investment) and technological progress (total factor productivity). Endogenous growth theory (Paul Romer): knowledge, human capital, and innovation drive sustained growth. Key: education quality, R&D spending, institutions, rule of law, and infrastructure.

18. The term 'PPP' (Purchasing Power Parity) is used to:

  1. Compare GDP across countries by adjusting for differences in price levels — at PPP, India is the 3rd largest economy (after USA and China, overtook Japan in 2019)
  2. Measure stock market performance
  3. Determine exchange rates only (market rate)
  4. Measure inflation

Answer: Compare GDP across countries by adjusting for differences in price levels — at PPP, India is the 3rd largest economy (after USA and China, overtook Japan in 2019)

Explanation: PPP: converts GDP using exchange rates that equalize purchasing power (prices of identical goods). Eliminates price level differences between countries for fair comparison. India's GDP at market exchange rate: ~$3.7 trillion (5th globally). At PPP: ~$13+ trillion (3rd globally after USA and China). PPP reflects actual living standards better.

19. India's services sector contributes approximately what percentage to GDP?

  1. About 55-57% of GDP — India has a services-led economy (IT, financial services, trade, tourism, telecom); followed by industry ~27% and agriculture ~17%
  2. 25%
  3. 75%
  4. 50%

Answer: About 55-57% of GDP — India has a services-led economy (IT, financial services, trade, tourism, telecom); followed by industry ~27% and agriculture ~17%

Explanation: India's GDP composition (2022-23): Services ~55% (IT/ITES, banking, insurance, real estate, trade, transport, tourism, education, health). Industry ~27% (manufacturing, construction, mining). Agriculture ~17% (but employs ~45% of workforce — showing massive productivity gap). India's IT exports (~$250 billion) make it the global IT capital.

20. The National Income Accounting identity (Expenditure method) GDP = C + I + G + (X – M) where X-M is:

  1. Net Exports (Exports minus Imports) — also called trade balance; for India, X-M is negative (India imports more than it exports in goods)
  2. Net investment
  3. Government expenditure
  4. Total consumption

Answer: Net Exports (Exports minus Imports) — also called trade balance; for India, X-M is negative (India imports more than it exports in goods)

Explanation: GDP Expenditure Method: C (Private Final Consumption Expenditure, ~57% of India's GDP) + I (Gross Fixed Capital Formation/Investments, ~30%) + G (Government Final Consumption Expenditure, ~11%) + (X-M) Net Exports (~-2 to -3% for India — trade deficit). Also: Income Method and Production/Output Method give same result.

21. The 'demographic transition' model describes:

  1. Four stages of population change from high birth/death rates (pre-industrial) to low birth/death rates (post-industrial) — most developing countries are in stage 2-3 (high birth, declining death rates)
  2. Immigration and emigration patterns
  3. Urban-rural population movement
  4. Only mortality rate changes

Answer: Four stages of population change from high birth/death rates (pre-industrial) to low birth/death rates (post-industrial) — most developing countries are in stage 2-3 (high birth, declining death rates)

Explanation: Demographic Transition Model (Warren Thompson, 1929): Stage 1: high birth + high death rates (pre-industrial, stable population). Stage 2: high birth + declining death (rapid population growth — India in this stage since 1950s). Stage 3: declining birth + low death (slowing growth). Stage 4: low birth + low death (stable, developed countries).

22. The 'multiplier-accelerator interaction' in Keynesian economics explains:

  1. Business cycles: initial investment increases income (multiplier effect) → increased income raises demand for capital goods (accelerator effect) → further investment → cycles of expansion and contraction
  2. Only inflation
  3. Only unemployment
  4. Only exchange rate fluctuations

Answer: Business cycles: initial investment increases income (multiplier effect) → increased income raises demand for capital goods (accelerator effect) → further investment → cycles of expansion and contraction

Explanation: Keynesian business cycle theory: Multiplier (ΔY = k × ΔI) + Accelerator (ΔI = v × ΔY): initial autonomous investment → income multiplied by k → higher income accelerates further investment → boom (expansion). When income growth slows, acceleration reverses, investment falls, income multiplier works downward → recession. Paul Samuelson formalized this interaction.

23. India's tax-to-GDP ratio compared to OECD countries is:

  1. Low (~11% of GDP for India vs ~34% OECD average) — low tax base due to: large informal economy, agricultural income exemption, tax evasion, low per capita income
  2. Higher than OECD
  3. Same as OECD
  4. Not measurable

Answer: Low (~11% of GDP for India vs ~34% OECD average) — low tax base due to: large informal economy, agricultural income exemption, tax evasion, low per capita income

Explanation: India's tax-to-GDP ratio: ~10.6-11% (direct+indirect). OECD average: ~34%. Key reasons for India's low ratio: large informal/unorganized sector (not in tax net), exemption of agricultural income, narrow direct tax base (~7 crore filers for 140 crore population), reliance on indirect taxes (which are regressive). GST implementation improved indirect tax collection.

24. 'Stagflation-proof' growth refers to:

  1. Economic growth accompanied by stable/low inflation and employment — sought by all economies but difficult during supply shocks (oil embargoes, COVID)
  2. Only agricultural growth
  3. Only IT sector growth
  4. Export-led growth only

Answer: Economic growth accompanied by stable/low inflation and employment — sought by all economies but difficult during supply shocks (oil embargoes, COVID)

Explanation: Ideally, economies want: high economic growth + low inflation + low unemployment. However, this is difficult due to trade-offs (Phillips Curve, inflationary pressures from growth). Supply-side shocks (oil price spikes, pandemics) cause both recession and inflation (stagflation). Structural reforms (improving productivity) can achieve growth with moderate inflation.

25. The PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan was launched in:

  1. October 2021 — GIS-based platform integrating infrastructure plans of 16 ministries (roads, railways, ports, airports, waterways, logistics) for coordinated infrastructure development
  2. 2015
  3. 2018
  4. 2019

Answer: October 2021 — GIS-based platform integrating infrastructure plans of 16 ministries (roads, railways, ports, airports, waterways, logistics) for coordinated infrastructure development

Explanation: PM Gati Shakti (October 2021): national master plan to bring about integrated, seamless, multi-modal connectivity. Digital platform integrating 16 ministries: Roads, Railways, Waterways, Aviation, Ports, Mass Transport, Logistics, Power, Pipelines, Telecom. Eliminates siloed planning; identifies bottlenecks. Aim: reduce logistics costs (currently ~14% of GDP vs global benchmark ~8%).
