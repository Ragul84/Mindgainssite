26. The term 'Sunset Clause' in economic policies means:

  1. An automatic expiry date built into the legislation/policy — if not renewed, policy lapses; common in tax incentives (e.g., SEZ tax benefits sunset clause), emergency measures, and financial regulations
  2. Policy that starts at end of year
  3. Budget provision for last quarter
  4. Policy for senior citizens (sunset of life)

Answer: An automatic expiry date built into the legislation/policy — if not renewed, policy lapses; common in tax incentives (e.g., SEZ tax benefits sunset clause), emergency measures, and financial regulations

Explanation: Sunset Clause: built-in expiration of a law/provision — 'sun sets' on the policy after a fixed period unless explicitly renewed. Examples: SEZ income tax exemptions (10-year sunset), COVID-19 emergency measures, some import duty waivers. Forces review: policymakers must affirmatively re-authorize rather than let inertia continue policies. Promotes accountability.

27. The concept of 'moral economy' in the context of peasant societies (James Scott) refers to:

  1. Unwritten code where landlords/state must ensure subsistence for peasants in bad times — violation of this code triggers peasant rebellions; explains resistance to market pricing of food
  2. Commercial economy based on profit
  3. Government-planned economy
  4. Ethical business practices

Answer: Unwritten code where landlords/state must ensure subsistence for peasants in bad times — violation of this code triggers peasant rebellions; explains resistance to market pricing of food

Explanation: Moral Economy (James Scott, 'Moral Economy of the Peasant,' 1976): peasant societies have implicit social contract — in exchange for deference/taxes, elites ensure peasants' survival in bad years (insurance principle). When elites/colonial powers violate this by insisting on market prices (even in famines), peasants rebel. Applied to explain colonial-era famines and peasant revolts in Asia.

28. 'Market failure' occurs when:

  1. Free markets fail to allocate resources efficiently — caused by: externalities, public goods, information asymmetry, monopoly power, missing markets — justifies government intervention
  2. Market prices are too high
  3. Too many sellers exist
  4. Government doesn't spend enough

Answer: Free markets fail to allocate resources efficiently — caused by: externalities, public goods, information asymmetry, monopoly power, missing markets — justifies government intervention

Explanation: Market Failure: situations where free markets don't achieve Pareto efficiency. Types: (1) Externalities (pollution, education spillovers), (2) Public goods (non-excludable, non-rival: defence, lighthouses), (3) Information asymmetry (adverse selection, moral hazard), (4) Market power (monopoly), (5) Missing markets (insurance for some risks). Government intervention: taxes, subsidies, regulation, direct provision.

29. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (15th FC) recommended which tax devolution share to states?

  1. 41% of the divisible pool (reduced from 14th FC's 42% because J&K bifurcated into 2 Union Territories — Ladakh's share retained at Centre) — for 2021-26 period
  2. 50% to states
  3. 35% to states
  4. Same as 14th FC (42%)

Answer: 41% of the divisible pool (reduced from 14th FC's 42% because J&K bifurcated into 2 Union Territories — Ladakh's share retained at Centre) — for 2021-26 period

Explanation: 15th Finance Commission (NK Singh, 2021-26): recommended 41% of divisible pool to states (1% less than 14th FC's 42%). Reason: J&K became 2 UTs — the 1% that was going to J&K state now goes to central government for UTs. Grants: Rs 10.3 lakh crore to states. Performance-based grants for health, education, urban bodies, PRIs.

30. India's 'Sovereign Green Bonds' were first issued in:

  1. January 2023 — Rs 16,000 crore raised in two tranches — proceeds used for green infrastructure, renewable energy, clean transport — India's first sovereign green bonds
  2. 2020
  3. 2015
  4. 2010

Answer: January 2023 — Rs 16,000 crore raised in two tranches — proceeds used for green infrastructure, renewable energy, clean transport — India's first sovereign green bonds

Explanation: India's Sovereign Green Bonds (SGBs — not to be confused with Sovereign Gold Bonds): first issued January 2023. Framework: proceeds for eligible green expenditures — renewable energy (solar, wind), green buildings, clean transport, water, biodiversity. Rs 8,000 crore in two tranches. 10-year and 5-year tenure. Part of India's commitment to achieving net-zero by 2070.

31. 'Cooperative federalism' in India refers to:

  1. Centre and States working together collaboratively — GST Council, Finance Commission, NITI Aayog's Team India Hub, joint schemes (Centrally Sponsored Schemes) represent cooperative elements
  2. States having full autonomy
  3. Centre having all powers
  4. Competing federalism between states

Answer: Centre and States working together collaboratively — GST Council, Finance Commission, NITI Aayog's Team India Hub, joint schemes (Centrally Sponsored Schemes) represent cooperative elements

Explanation: Cooperative Federalism: Centre-State partnership and cooperation. Examples: GST Council (Centre+States jointly decide tax rates), Finance Commission (balances Centre-State fiscal needs), NITI Aayog (PM+CMs meet as Governing Council), CSS (Centrally Sponsored Schemes: Centre+State funding). Competitive federalism: states competing for investment ('ease of doing business' rankings).

32. Capital expenditure in the Union Budget includes:

  1. Expenditure that creates assets or reduces liabilities — infrastructure (roads, railways), defence capital, loans to states, capital injection in PSUs, repayment of government debt
  2. Current spending on salaries and subsidies (revenue expenditure)
  3. Tax refunds
  4. Social sector grants

Answer: Expenditure that creates assets or reduces liabilities — infrastructure (roads, railways), defence capital, loans to states, capital injection in PSUs, repayment of government debt

Explanation: Capital Expenditure (Capex): creates assets or reduces liabilities. Components: Infrastructure (roads, railways, defence equipment), Loans to states and PSUs, Capital infusion in public sector banks, Repayment of public debt. India's Capex in Union Budget: ~Rs 10 lakh crore (2023-24). 'Effective Capex' includes grants-in-aid to states for capex. High capex → productive capacity building.

33. The concept of 'negative income tax' was proposed by:

  1. Milton Friedman (1962) — at incomes below a threshold, government pays individuals (negative tax) instead of collecting; combines welfare and taxes into one system — inspired Universal Basic Income ideas
  2. John Keynes
  3. Paul Samuelson
  4. Arthur Laffer

Answer: Milton Friedman (1962) — at incomes below a threshold, government pays individuals (negative tax) instead of collecting; combines welfare and taxes into one system — inspired Universal Basic Income ideas

Explanation: Negative Income Tax (Friedman, 'Capitalism and Freedom,' 1962): replace complex welfare bureaucracy with a simple system — at incomes below a threshold, government pays subsidies; above threshold, people pay taxes. Set the threshold and rate carefully. More efficient than targeted programs. Inspired UBI concepts. US pilot experiments in 1970s showed some reduction in work effort.

34. India's 'Special Category States' historically included:

  1. 11 states given additional central assistance (90% grants, 10% loans for CSS) — NE states (8), Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Jammu & Kashmir — recognized in 1969 based on Gadgil formula for remoteness, hilly terrain, low economic base
  2. Only northeastern states
  3. All Indian states
  4. Only southern states

Answer: 11 states given additional central assistance (90% grants, 10% loans for CSS) — NE states (8), Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Jammu & Kashmir — recognized in 1969 based on Gadgil formula for remoteness, hilly terrain, low economic base

Explanation: Special Category States (SCS): 1969 National Development Council. Criteria: hilly/difficult terrain, low economic base, international borders, sparsely populated. 11 SCS: Arunachal, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Tripura, Sikkim, HP, Uttarakhand, J&K (now UT). Benefits: 90:10 central funding for CSS (vs 60:40 normal). After 14th FC, special category distinction de-emphasized (higher tax devolution to all).

35. India's 'Integrated Goods and Services Tax' (IGST) applies to:

  1. Inter-state supply of goods and services and imports — collected by Centre, then transferred to consuming state (as SGST component) and Centre retains CGST component — prevents 'origin state' capturing all revenue
  2. All transactions (that's CGST+SGST)
  3. Only exports
  4. International transactions only

Answer: Inter-state supply of goods and services and imports — collected by Centre, then transferred to consuming state (as SGST component) and Centre retains CGST component — prevents 'origin state' capturing all revenue

Explanation: GST types: CGST (Central GST): intra-state, Centre's share. SGST (State GST): intra-state, State's share. IGST (Integrated GST): inter-state supply + imports — collected by Centre, then SGST portion transferred to consuming state. Mechanism: prevents tax cascading on inter-state trade and resolves origin-vs-destination revenue debate. Input tax credit (ITC) flows seamlessly across CGST-IGST-SGST.

36. 'Goods and Services Tax Network' (GSTN) is:

  1. Technology platform managing GST — non-government company (Government holds 49%, financial institutions/banks hold 51%) — handles GST registration, return filing, payment, refunds, and data analytics
  2. A government ministry
  3. Part of Finance Ministry
  4. RBI subsidiary

Answer: Technology platform managing GST — non-government company (Government holds 49%, financial institutions/banks hold 51%) — handles GST registration, return filing, payment, refunds, and data analytics

Explanation: GSTN (2013): non-government, non-profit, Section 8 company. Shareholders: Central Govt (20%), State Govts (20% together), financial institutions (HDFC, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, NSE Strategic Investments, LIC Housing Finance: 60%). Provides IT infrastructure for GST: taxpayer registration, return filing (GSTR-1, GSTR-3B), invoice matching, input tax credit reconciliation, refund processing, analytics.

37. India's 'Agri-SURE' (Agriculture Startup Fund) was announced in:

  1. 2023 Union Budget — Rs 2,200 crore fund under NABARD for supporting agricultural startups, rural enterprises, and agri-tech companies in underserved areas
  2. 2015
  3. 2018
  4. 2010

Answer: 2023 Union Budget — Rs 2,200 crore fund under NABARD for supporting agricultural startups, rural enterprises, and agri-tech companies in underserved areas

Explanation: Agri SURE (Agriculture Scale-Up through Rural Enterprises) Fund: Rs 2,200 crore fund announced in Union Budget 2023-24. Managed by NABARD. Provides financial support to agricultural startups and rural enterprises particularly in farm equipment, precision agriculture, post-harvest management, and agri-tech. Complementary to Startup India and PMKSY.

38. The Contingency Fund of India is kept at the disposal of:

  1. Parliament
  2. Finance Commission
  3. Prime Minister
  4. President of India

Answer: President of India

Explanation: The Contingency Fund of India (Article 267) is at the President's disposal for urgent unforeseen expenditures. Its size is Rs 500 crore; Parliament must ratify later.

39. A tax where the rate increases as the taxable amount increases is called:

  1. Regressive tax
  2. Proportional tax
  3. Progressive tax
  4. Flat tax

Answer: Progressive tax

Explanation: Progressive tax: higher income → higher tax rate. India's personal income tax uses progressive slabs. Regressive taxes (like indirect taxes) burden lower incomes more.

40. Tax incidence refers to:

  1. The tax rate applied
  2. Who legally pays the tax
  3. Who ultimately bears the economic burden of the tax
  4. The amount of tax collected

Answer: Who ultimately bears the economic burden of the tax

Explanation: Tax incidence = who ultimately bears the tax burden after shifting. Legal incidence (statutory) differs from economic incidence. E.g., GST is legally on seller but shifted to consumer.

41. The Pigouvian tax is designed to correct:

  1. Income inequality
  2. Negative externalities (social costs)
  3. Public goods under-provision
  4. Natural monopolies

Answer: Negative externalities (social costs)

Explanation: Pigouvian tax (A.C. Pigou): tax equal to marginal external cost to correct negative externalities (pollution). Makes private cost = social cost. Carbon tax is a Pigouvian tax.

42. The 'Washington Consensus' — a set of economic policy prescriptions — was coined by economist:

  1. Paul Krugman
  2. Joseph Stiglitz
  3. John Williamson
  4. Jeffrey Sachs

Answer: John Williamson

Explanation: John Williamson (1989) coined 'Washington Consensus' — 10 prescriptions: fiscal discipline, tax reform, trade liberalization, FDI openness, privatization, deregulation. Controversial for developing nations.

43. Deadweight loss in economics refers to:

  1. Direct cost of taxes collected
  2. Loss of economic efficiency when equilibrium is not achieved (lost surplus)
  3. Government spending on welfare
  4. Production costs that exceed revenue

Answer: Loss of economic efficiency when equilibrium is not achieved (lost surplus)

Explanation: Deadweight loss: reduction in total surplus (consumer + producer) due to market inefficiency caused by taxes, price controls, monopoly, or externalities. Represents foregone transactions.

44. The Economic Survey is presented in Parliament just before the Union Budget by:

  1. RBI Governor
  2. Chief Economic Adviser to the Government of India
  3. Finance Minister
  4. NITI Aayog Vice Chairman

Answer: Chief Economic Adviser to the Government of India

Explanation: Economic Survey: prepared by the Economic Division of Ministry of Finance under the Chief Economic Adviser (CEA). Reviews the economy's performance. Presented before Union Budget. Volume 1: thematic; Volume 2: data.

45. The Revenue Deficit is defined as:

  1. Total expenditure minus total receipts
  2. Revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts
  3. Fiscal deficit minus interest payments
  4. Capital expenditure minus capital receipts

Answer: Revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts

Explanation: Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts. Shows that current (non-capital) spending exceeds current income, meaning government borrows even for day-to-day expenses (not just investment). Negative for fiscal health.

46. Which authority approves the Union Budget in India?

  1. Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs
  2. Lok Sabha (lower house of Parliament)
  3. Both Houses of Parliament
  4. President of India

Answer: Lok Sabha (lower house of Parliament)

Explanation: Appropriation Bill and Finance Bill (forming Union Budget) must pass Lok Sabha — money bills are exclusively Lok Sabha's domain (Article 110). Rajya Sabha can only suggest amendments (no power to reject). President's assent follows.

47. Direct taxes are taxes paid directly by:

  1. The person/entity on whom it is levied (cannot be shifted) — Income Tax, Corporate Tax, Capital Gains Tax, Wealth Tax (abolished 2016)
  2. Consumer who passes it on (indirect taxes like GST, customs)
  3. Only businesses
  4. Only agricultural income

Answer: The person/entity on whom it is levied (cannot be shifted) — Income Tax, Corporate Tax, Capital Gains Tax, Wealth Tax (abolished 2016)

Explanation: Direct Taxes: levied on and collected from the same person (incidence and impact on same entity). Income Tax (individuals), Corporate Tax, Capital Gains Tax, Securities Transaction Tax. Administered by CBDT (Central Board of Direct Taxes). Indirect Taxes: GST, customs duty — taxpayer passes burden to consumer. Administered by CBIC.

48. The Twelfth Finance Commission of India recommended:

  1. That Finance Commission is appointed every 5 years under Article 280 to recommend distribution of central taxes between Centre and States; 15th FC (2021-26) recommended 41% share for states
  2. Only taxation policy
  3. Banking regulation
  4. Trade policy

Answer: That Finance Commission is appointed every 5 years under Article 280 to recommend distribution of central taxes between Centre and States; 15th FC (2021-26) recommended 41% share for states

Explanation: Finance Commission (Article 280): constituted every 5 years to recommend: (1) distribution of tax revenue between Centre and States (vertical devolution), (2) distribution among states (horizontal devolution), (3) grants-in-aid to states. 15th FC (N.K. Singh, 2021-26): recommended 41% of divisible pool to states. 14th FC: 42%.

49. India's tax administration reforms include:

  1. Faceless Assessment (2019): random assignment of cases, no taxpayer-AO interaction; Taxpayer Charter; DIN (Document Identification Number) on all communications; pre-filled ITR; Vivad se Vishwas scheme for dispute resolution
  2. Only GST introduction
  3. Only new taxes
  4. Only income tax reduction

Answer: Faceless Assessment (2019): random assignment of cases, no taxpayer-AO interaction; Taxpayer Charter; DIN (Document Identification Number) on all communications; pre-filled ITR; Vivad se Vishwas scheme for dispute resolution

Explanation: Income Tax reforms under 'Transparent Taxation — Honouring the Honest' (2020): Faceless Assessment (all appeals and assessments done online, randomly allocated, no geographic/taxpayer-officer nexus), Taxpayer Charter (rights and responsibilities defined), DIN on every tax notice/letter (verifiable online), pre-filled ITR forms, Vivad se Vishwas (2020, settling 4.8 lakh tax disputes of Rs 9.32 lakh crore).

50. PM CARES Fund (Prime Minister's Citizen Assistance and Relief in Emergency Situations Fund) was established in:

  1. January 2020
  2. March 2020
  3. June 2020
  4. December 2019

Answer: March 2020

Explanation: PM CARES Fund was established on March 28, 2020 to deal with COVID-19 pandemic. It is a public charitable trust; donations are tax-exempt under 80G. Separate from PM National Relief Fund.
