26. What is 'opportunity cost' in economics?

  1. The value of the next best alternative foregone when making a choice — e.g., opportunity cost of studying = income from working
  2. The actual monetary cost of a good
  3. The cost of raw materials
  4. The labor cost of production

Answer: The value of the next best alternative foregone when making a choice — e.g., opportunity cost of studying = income from working

Explanation: Opportunity Cost: value of the best alternative foregone when a choice is made. Example: if you spend Rs 1 lakh on a business and it could have earned 8% in a bank (Rs 8,000), then opportunity cost = Rs 8,000/year. Fundamental to economics — every decision involves opportunity cost. Key to understanding rational decision-making.

  1. Reserve Bank of India (RBI) — held in gold, foreign currency assets (mainly USD T-bills), SDRs, and reserve tranche at IMF; ~$640 billion (2024)
  2. Finance Ministry
  3. SEBI
  4. EXIM Bank

Answer: Reserve Bank of India (RBI) — held in gold, foreign currency assets (mainly USD T-bills), SDRs, and reserve tranche at IMF; ~$640 billion (2024)

Explanation: RBI manages India's foreign exchange reserves: Foreign Currency Assets (FCA, largest component — USD, EUR, GBP, JPY treasury bills and bonds), Gold reserves (~800 tons), Special Drawing Rights (SDRs) from IMF, and Reserve Tranche Position at IMF. Used to: stabilize rupee, manage BoP, instill confidence. India: 4th largest forex reserves globally.

29. The term 'Laissez-Faire' in economics means:

  1. Let it be/do — minimal government intervention; markets self-regulate through price mechanism (Adam Smith's 'invisible hand')
  2. Heavy government control
  3. Protectionist trade policy
  4. Mixed economy

Answer: Let it be/do — minimal government intervention; markets self-regulate through price mechanism (Adam Smith's 'invisible hand')

Explanation: Laissez-faire (French: 'let do/let go'): economic philosophy advocating minimal government intervention. Markets, through price mechanism ('invisible hand' — Adam Smith, 'Wealth of Nations,' 1776), efficiently allocate resources. Associated with classical economics. Opposite: interventionism/planned economy.

33. The term 'circular economy' refers to:

  1. An economic system that eliminates waste by reusing, repairing, refurbishing, and recycling materials — contrasts with linear economy (take-make-dispose)
  2. Economic cycle of boom and bust
  3. Circular flow of income in national accounting
  4. Trade circle between countries

Answer: An economic system that eliminates waste by reusing, repairing, refurbishing, and recycling materials — contrasts with linear economy (take-make-dispose)

Explanation: Circular Economy: regenerative system that keeps resources in use for as long as possible. Principles: reduce consumption, reuse products, recycle materials, recover energy. Contrasts with linear (take-make-waste) economy. UN SDGs include circular economy elements. EU's Circular Economy Action Plan (2020). Key to achieving net-zero and sustainable development.

Improved India's ranking in World Bank's Ease of Doing Business: Resolving Insolvency indicator improved dramatically.

36. The term 'quantitative easing' (QE) refers to:

  1. Central bank buys government securities or other financial assets from market to inject money into the economy — used when interest rates are near zero (unconventional monetary policy)
  2. Increasing bank reserve requirements
  3. Raising interest rates
  4. Reducing government spending

Answer: Central bank buys government securities or other financial assets from market to inject money into the economy — used when interest rates are near zero (unconventional monetary policy)

Explanation: QE: when conventional monetary policy (lowering interest rates) is insufficient (near-zero rates), central bank creates money and buys assets (government bonds, MBS) from banks. This: increases banks' reserves, lowers long-term interest rates, boosts credit, and stimulates economy. Used by US Fed, ECB, Bank of Japan after 2008 financial crisis.

38. The term 'moral hazard' in economics refers to:

  1. When an entity takes on more risk because it is protected from consequences (e.g., bank taking risky loans knowing government will bail it out; insured person being less careful)
  2. Unethical business practices
  3. Market manipulation
  4. Tax evasion

Answer: When an entity takes on more risk because it is protected from consequences (e.g., bank taking risky loans knowing government will bail it out; insured person being less careful)

Explanation: Moral hazard: tendency to take on more risk when protected from the consequences (risk is transferred to another party). Examples: bank risky lending (expecting government bailout — 'too big to fail'), insured driving recklessly, principal-agent problem (employee working less hard without monitoring). Key concept in insurance economics, banking regulation.

39. The term 'natural monopoly' refers to:

  1. Industry where one firm can produce output most efficiently (lowest cost) due to very high fixed costs — e.g., railways, electricity distribution, water supply, pipelines
  2. Monopoly given by nature/geography
  3. Monopoly on natural resources
  4. Government monopoly only

Answer: Industry where one firm can produce output most efficiently (lowest cost) due to very high fixed costs — e.g., railways, electricity distribution, water supply, pipelines

Explanation: Natural Monopoly: occurs when average cost declines continuously with scale (economies of scale so large that one firm serves the whole market more efficiently than multiple competitors). Examples: electricity grid, railway tracks, water pipes, telecom infrastructure. Typically regulated by government (or government-owned) to prevent monopoly pricing.

43. The 'Dutch Auction' method used for government bond sales means:

  1. All winning bidders pay the lowest price (cut-off price) that clears the auction — price-competitive auction method used by RBI for T-bills and dated securities
  2. Everyone pays their own bid price (multiple price auction)
  3. Government fixes the price beforehand
  4. Only one bidder wins

Answer: All winning bidders pay the lowest price (cut-off price) that clears the auction — price-competitive auction method used by RBI for T-bills and dated securities

Explanation: Dutch Auction (uniform price auction): bids arranged highest to lowest; securities allocated from highest bidders down until total issue amount exhausted; all successful bidders pay the cut-off price (lowest accepted bid). Used by RBI for G-Secs. Alternative: French Auction (multiple price) — each bidder pays own bid price.

44. The term 'crowding out' in economics refers to:

  1. Government borrowing raises interest rates → reduces private investment (government 'crowds out' private spending) — main criticism of Keynesian stimulus in classical economics
  2. Unemployment crowding out employed workers
  3. Population density effects
  4. Export crowding out domestic consumption

Answer: Government borrowing raises interest rates → reduces private investment (government 'crowds out' private spending) — main criticism of Keynesian stimulus in classical economics

Explanation: Crowding Out: when government increases spending/borrowing → demands more funds from financial markets → interest rates rise → private investment becomes more expensive and falls. Classical economists worry about this effect of fiscal stimulus. Counter-argument (Keynesians): in recession, resources are underutilized, so government spending need not crowd out private investment.

45. The term 'deflation' means:

  1. Sustained decrease in the general price level (negative inflation rate) — can lead to deflationary spiral: falling prices → less production → unemployment → less demand → further price fall
  2. Temporary price decrease
  3. Decrease in value of currency abroad
  4. Reduction in money supply without price effects

Answer: Sustained decrease in the general price level (negative inflation rate) — can lead to deflationary spiral: falling prices → less production → unemployment → less demand → further price fall

Explanation: Deflation: persistent fall in general price level. Causes: demand-side collapse, excess supply, tight monetary policy. Dangers: deflationary spiral (debt-deflation: real debt burden rises), reduced investment (prices expected to fall further), falling wages, rising unemployment. Japan's 'lost decade' (1990s-2000s) is example. Central banks try to avoid deflation.

46. Index of Industrial Production (IIP) measures:

  1. Monthly changes in the volume of production of industrial goods across mining, manufacturing, and electricity sectors — base year 2011-12
  2. Annual GDP growth
  3. Agricultural production
  4. Service sector output

Answer: Monthly changes in the volume of production of industrial goods across mining, manufacturing, and electricity sectors — base year 2011-12

Explanation: IIP (Index of Industrial Production): compiled by National Statistical Office (NSO). Measures changes in industrial output (mining: ~14%, manufacturing: ~78%, electricity: ~8% by weight). Base year: 2011-12. Released monthly (with 6-week lag). Key indicator for industrial health and monetary policy decisions.

  2. Finance Ministry

49. The Revenue Deficit in the Union Budget is:

  1. Excess of Revenue Expenditure over Revenue Receipts — shows government is borrowing to meet day-to-day expenses, not creating assets — target: zero revenue deficit
  2. Total government borrowing
  3. Excess of total expenditure over total revenue
  4. Export-import imbalance

Answer: Excess of Revenue Expenditure over Revenue Receipts — shows government is borrowing to meet day-to-day expenses, not creating assets — target: zero revenue deficit

Explanation: Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure - Revenue Receipts. Revenue expenditure: salaries, interest payments, subsidies, defence revenue spending (day-to-day costs, no asset creation). Revenue receipts: tax + non-tax revenues. Fiscal Responsibility: eliminate revenue deficit (target from FRBM). Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit minus Interest Payments.

50. The economic concept 'invisible hand' was given by:

  1. Adam Smith (in 'Wealth of Nations,' 1776) — the idea that individuals pursuing self-interest in free markets unintentionally promote public good through competition
  2. John Maynard Keynes
  3. Karl Marx
  4. Milton Friedman

Answer: Adam Smith (in 'Wealth of Nations,' 1776) — the idea that individuals pursuing self-interest in free markets unintentionally promote public good through competition

Explanation: Adam Smith's 'invisible hand': when individuals act in their own self-interest (profit motive) in competitive markets, they are guided as if by an 'invisible hand' to produce goods/services society wants at efficient prices. Basis of free-market capitalism. Wealth of Nations (1776) also argued for division of labor, free trade, and against mercantilism.
