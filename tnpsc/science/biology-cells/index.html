Biology Cells MCQs with answers

Use these 25 worked Biology Cells questions to revise the TNPSC Science syllabus. Each answer includes an explanation; the full practice set contains 70 questions.

1. Which gas do humans breathe in to survive?

  1. Carbon Dioxide
  2. Nitrogen
  3. Oxygen
  4. Hydrogen

Answer: Oxygen

Explanation: Humans (and most living organisms) need oxygen to carry out cellular respiration and produce energy.

2. What is the function of the heart in the human body?

  1. Digest food
  2. Pump blood throughout the body
  3. Filter waste from blood
  4. Produce hormones

Answer: Pump blood throughout the body

Explanation: The heart is a muscular organ that pumps blood through the circulatory system, supplying oxygen and nutrients to all body cells.

3. The basic unit of life is the:

  1. Tissue
  2. Organ
  3. Cell
  4. Atom

Answer: Cell

Explanation: The cell is the basic structural, functional, and biological unit of all living organisms.

4. The powerhouse of the cell is:

  1. Nucleus
  2. Mitochondria
  3. Ribosome
  4. Chloroplast

Answer: Mitochondria

Explanation: Mitochondria are called the 'powerhouse of the cell' because they produce ATP (energy) through cellular respiration.

5. Which blood group is called the 'universal donor'?

  1. A+
  2. B+
  3. O-
  4. AB+

Answer: O-

Explanation: O- (O negative) is the universal donor for red blood cells as it can be given to patients of any blood group.

6. Which hormone is produced by the pancreas to control blood sugar?

  1. Adrenaline
  2. Thyroxine
  3. Insulin
  4. Oestrogen

Answer: Insulin

Explanation: Insulin is produced by the beta cells of the pancreatic islets. It regulates blood glucose by allowing cells to take up glucose. Deficiency of insulin causes diabetes.

7. The number of chromosomes in a normal human body cell is:

  1. 23
  2. 44
  3. 46
  4. 48

Answer: 46

Explanation: Normal human body cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs). Sperm and egg cells have 23 chromosomes each (haploid).

8. The basic structural and functional unit of life is:

  1. Cell
  2. Tissue
  3. Organ
  4. Organism

Answer: Cell

Explanation: The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms. Cell theory (Schleiden and Schwann, 1838-39): all living things are made of cells; the cell is the basic unit of life.

9. The human brain has how many neurons (nerve cells) approximately?

  1. About 86 billion neurons
  2. 1 million neurons
  3. 1 billion neurons
  4. 100 trillion neurons

Answer: About 86 billion neurons

Explanation: The human brain has approximately 86 billion neurons (nerve cells) — about as many as stars in the Milky Way. Each neuron can form up to 10,000 synaptic connections, creating 100 trillion connections total.

10. Hydrogen is unique because:

  1. It can act as both a metal (alkali metal-like) and non-metal (halogen-like) — placed separately in periodic table
  2. It has only 1 neutron
  3. It is always a gas
  4. It is always radioactive

Answer: It can act as both a metal (alkali metal-like) and non-metal (halogen-like) — placed separately in periodic table

Explanation: Hydrogen (H) is unique: 1 electron, 1 proton. It can lose its electron (like alkali metals, forming H⁺) or gain one (like halogens, forming H⁻). Future clean fuel source (fuel cells).

11. Cloning is the process of producing:

  1. Genetically identical copies of an organism from a single cell (asexual reproduction)
  2. Hybrid organisms
  3. Genetically modified organisms
  4. Embryo splitting only

Answer: Genetically identical copies of an organism from a single cell (asexual reproduction)

Explanation: Cloning produces genetically identical organisms. Dolly the sheep (1996) was the first mammal cloned from an adult somatic cell (via nuclear transfer into an enucleated egg). Therapeutic cloning for stem cells is research topic.

12. Vaccines work by:

  1. Stimulating the immune system to produce antibodies without causing full disease (immunological memory)
  2. Killing bacteria directly
  3. Providing ready-made antibodies
  4. Reducing body temperature

Answer: Stimulating the immune system to produce antibodies without causing full disease (immunological memory)

Explanation: Vaccines contain weakened/killed pathogens, toxoids, or specific antigens. They stimulate the immune system to produce antibodies and memory cells. On future exposure to the real pathogen, the immune system responds rapidly.

13. The basic functional unit of the nervous system is:

  1. Neuron (nerve cell) — with dendrites, cell body, and axon
  2. Glial cell
  3. Synapse
  4. Myelin sheath

Answer: Neuron (nerve cell) — with dendrites, cell body, and axon

Explanation: A neuron is the basic structural and functional unit of the nervous system. It has: dendrites (receive signals), cell body/soma (processes signals), and axon (transmits signals). Signals travel as electrical impulses.

14. The gap between two neurons where nerve impulses are transmitted is called:

  1. Synapse (chemicals called neurotransmitters cross this gap)
  2. Axon
  3. Myelin
  4. Node of Ranvier

Answer: Synapse (chemicals called neurotransmitters cross this gap)

Explanation: A synapse is the junction between two neurons (or neuron and muscle). Nerve impulses trigger release of neurotransmitters (e.g., acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin) from the presynaptic terminal, which bind to receptors on the postsynaptic cell.

15. Testosterone is produced by the:

  1. Testes (in males) and small amounts by adrenal cortex — responsible for male secondary sexual characteristics
  2. Ovaries
  3. Thyroid gland
  4. Adrenal medulla

Answer: Testes (in males) and small amounts by adrenal cortex — responsible for male secondary sexual characteristics

Explanation: Testosterone is the primary male sex hormone, produced by Leydig cells in the testes. It is responsible for: male secondary sexual characteristics (beard, deep voice, muscle mass), sperm production, and bone density. Women also produce small amounts in ovaries/adrenal glands.

16. Platelets (thrombocytes) are responsible for:

  1. Blood clotting (stopping bleeding at wound sites by forming a clot)
  2. Carrying oxygen
  3. Fighting infection
  4. Producing antibodies

Answer: Blood clotting (stopping bleeding at wound sites by forming a clot)

Explanation: Platelets (thrombocytes) are small cell fragments that clump at wound sites and release clotting factors, leading to formation of a fibrin clot. Normal platelet count: 1.5-4 lakh per microliter. Low platelet count (thrombocytopenia) occurs in dengue.

17. Mendel's Law of Segregation states that:

  1. Two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation — each gamete carries only one allele for each trait
  2. Traits blend together
  3. Dominant allele always shows
  4. All genes are inherited together

Answer: Two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation — each gamete carries only one allele for each trait

Explanation: Mendel's First Law: alleles (two copies of each gene in diploid organisms) segregate (separate) during gamete (sex cell) formation. Each gamete gets only one allele. At fertilization, offspring inherit one allele from each parent. Verified by Punnett square.

18. Viruses differ from bacteria in that viruses:

  1. Are not living cells — they are non-cellular; lack own metabolism; can only replicate inside host cells; are much smaller
  2. Are cells with nucleus
  3. Can grow outside host
  4. Have their own ribosomes for protein synthesis

Answer: Are not living cells — they are non-cellular; lack own metabolism; can only replicate inside host cells; are much smaller

Explanation: Viruses are non-cellular (acellular) — just genetic material (DNA or RNA) enclosed in a protein coat (capsid). They cannot replicate or metabolize independently — they hijack host cell machinery. Viruses are ~10-300 nm (smaller than bacteria, 0.5-5 µm). Antibiotics don't work on viruses.

19. The antibiotic penicillin works by:

  1. Inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis (bacteria lyse due to osmotic pressure without the wall) — does NOT affect human cells
  2. Killing viruses
  3. Disrupting DNA replication
  4. Blocking protein synthesis directly

Answer: Inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis (bacteria lyse due to osmotic pressure without the wall) — does NOT affect human cells

Explanation: Penicillin (and other beta-lactam antibiotics) inhibit enzymes needed to form cross-links in the bacterial cell wall (peptidoglycan). Without the wall, bacteria burst from osmotic pressure. Human cells have no cell wall, so penicillin is safe. Alexander Fleming discovered it in 1928.

20. Cancer is caused by:

  1. Uncontrolled division of abnormal cells (due to mutations in genes controlling cell cycle — oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes)
  2. Bacterial infection
  3. Viral infection only
  4. Nutritional deficiency

Answer: Uncontrolled division of abnormal cells (due to mutations in genes controlling cell cycle — oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes)

Explanation: Cancer: mutations in proto-oncogenes (become oncogenes promoting growth) or tumor suppressor genes (p53, Rb — normally prevent uncontrolled division) lead to uncontrolled cell growth. Causes: tobacco, UV radiation, some viruses (HPV, HBV), carcinogens, heredity.

21. The five-kingdom classification system was proposed by:

  1. Robert Whittaker (1969) — Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia
  2. Carolus Linnaeus (two-kingdom: Plants and Animals)
  3. Carl Woese (three-domain: Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya)
  4. Ernst Haeckel

Answer: Robert Whittaker (1969) — Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia

Explanation: Whittaker (1969) proposed 5 kingdoms: Monera (bacteria/prokaryotes), Protista (unicellular eukaryotes), Fungi, Plantae, Animalia. Carl Woese (1990) proposed 3 domains: Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya (most modern classification). Linnaeus (1758) established binomial nomenclature.

22. The exchange of gases in the lungs occurs in:

  1. Alveoli (tiny air sacs — ~700 million in lungs, total surface area ~70 m²) — O₂ enters blood, CO₂ exits
  2. Bronchi
  3. Trachea
  4. Diaphragm

Answer: Alveoli (tiny air sacs — ~700 million in lungs, total surface area ~70 m²) — O₂ enters blood, CO₂ exits

Explanation: Alveoli are microscopic air sacs surrounded by capillaries. O₂ diffuses from alveolar air into blood; CO₂ diffuses from blood into alveoli for exhalation. Large surface area (~70 m², size of a tennis court) and thin walls (1 cell thick) enable efficient gas exchange.

23. The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) in a cell is of two types:

  1. Rough ER (with ribosomes — protein synthesis and processing) and Smooth ER (no ribosomes — lipid synthesis, detoxification, Ca²⁺ storage)
  2. Nuclear ER and cytoplasmic ER
  3. Inner and outer ER
  4. Active and passive ER

Answer: Rough ER (with ribosomes — protein synthesis and processing) and Smooth ER (no ribosomes — lipid synthesis, detoxification, Ca²⁺ storage)

Explanation: Rough ER: has ribosomes on surface; synthesizes and processes proteins destined for export or membrane. Smooth ER: no ribosomes; synthesizes lipids/steroids, detoxifies drugs (liver SER), and stores calcium (muscle SER). ER is continuous with the nuclear envelope (perinuclear space).

24. Cellular respiration (aerobic) equation is:

  1. C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP (energy)
  2. 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
  3. C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂
  4. Glucose + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂ + energy

Answer: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP (energy)

Explanation: Aerobic cellular respiration: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + 38 ATP. Glucose is broken down using oxygen to release energy (ATP). CO₂ and water are by-products. Occurs in mitochondria.

25. The normal number of chromosomes in a human cell is:

  1. 46 chromosomes (23 pairs)
  2. 23 chromosomes
  3. 48 chromosomes
  4. 44 chromosomes

Answer: 46 chromosomes (23 pairs)

Explanation: Human cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs). Gametes (sperm, egg) have 23 chromosomes (haploid). Pair 23 determines sex: XX = female, XY = male. Down syndrome has 47 chromosomes (trisomy 21).
