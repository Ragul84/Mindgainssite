Banking Finance MCQs with answers

Use these 25 worked Banking Finance questions to revise the TNPSC Economy syllabus. Each answer includes an explanation; the full practice set contains 644 questions.

1. Which is the apex bank of India?

  1. State Bank of India
  2. Bank of India
  3. Reserve Bank of India
  4. Punjab National Bank

Answer: Reserve Bank of India

Explanation: RBI is the apex bank (central bank) of India, established in 1935. It regulates all commercial banks and controls monetary policy.

2. The headquarters of RBI is in:

  1. New Delhi
  2. Kolkata
  3. Mumbai
  4. Chennai

Answer: Mumbai

Explanation: The Reserve Bank of India's headquarters is in Mumbai (Maharashtra). It was originally established in Kolkata in 1935 and moved to Mumbai in 1937.

3. The 'Reverse Repo Rate' is:

  1. Rate at which RBI borrows from commercial banks
  2. Rate at which banks borrow from public
  3. Rate at which RBI lends to banks
  4. Rate at which banks lend to each other

Answer: Rate at which RBI borrows from commercial banks

Explanation: Reverse Repo Rate is the rate at which the RBI borrows money from commercial banks. It is always lower than the Repo Rate.

4. The CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio) means:

  1. The percentage of deposits banks must lend to industry
  2. The minimum percentage of deposits banks must maintain as cash with RBI
  3. The ratio of gold to currency
  4. The minimum capital banks must hold

Answer: The minimum percentage of deposits banks must maintain as cash with RBI

Explanation: CRR is the percentage of a bank's total deposits that must be maintained as cash reserves with the RBI. It controls money supply — higher CRR = less money available to lend.

5. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) was established in:

  1. 1935 (April 1) — under the RBI Act 1934; nationalized in 1949; headquartered in Mumbai
  2. 1947
  3. 1969
  4. 1950

Answer: 1935 (April 1) — under the RBI Act 1934; nationalized in 1949; headquartered in Mumbai

Explanation: RBI: established April 1, 1935, as a private shareholders' bank under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 (based on Hilton-Young Commission recommendations). Nationalized January 1, 1949. Governor: Shaktikanta Das (26th Governor). HQ: Mumbai. Key functions: monetary policy, currency issue, banking regulation, forex management.

6. The Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) requires banks to:

  1. Maintain a certain percentage of their NDTL in liquid assets — gold, cash, or approved government securities (currently 18%)
  2. Only maintain cash with RBI
  3. Pay tax to the government
  4. Keep a minimum balance with other banks

Answer: Maintain a certain percentage of their NDTL in liquid assets — gold, cash, or approved government securities (currently 18%)

Explanation: SLR: banks must hold a minimum % of their deposits (NDTL) in liquid assets: cash, gold, and approved securities (mainly government bonds). Current SLR: 18% (2024). This ensures banks always have liquid assets to meet customer demands. SLR also channels funds to government borrowing. SLR + CRR together limit banks' funds available for lending.

7. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) was established at:

  1. Bretton Woods Conference (1944), operative 1945 — provides financial assistance to countries facing balance of payments crises; HQ: Washington DC; 190 members
  2. 1950
  3. 1960
  4. Geneva, 1947

Answer: Bretton Woods Conference (1944), operative 1945 — provides financial assistance to countries facing balance of payments crises; HQ: Washington DC; 190 members

Explanation: IMF: established at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire (July 1944), alongside World Bank. Came into existence 1945, HQ: Washington DC. Functions: balance of payments assistance, macroeconomic surveillance, technical assistance. Special Drawing Rights (SDR) is IMF's reserve currency. India's largest borrowing from IMF: 1991 crisis ($2.2 billion, mortgaged 67 tons of gold).

8. The World Bank Group's main lending arm for developing countries is:

  1. IBRD (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development) — lends to middle-income countries; IDA (International Development Association) — concessional loans to poorest countries
  2. IMF
  3. WTO
  4. ADB

Answer: IBRD (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development) — lends to middle-income countries; IDA (International Development Association) — concessional loans to poorest countries

Explanation: World Bank Group (est. Bretton Woods, 1944): IBRD (1945): loans/guarantees to middle-income/creditworthy countries. IDA (1960): interest-free/low-interest loans to poorest countries (including India for many years, now graduated). IFC: private sector lending. MIGA: political risk insurance. HQ: Washington DC. India is the largest borrower from World Bank.

9. The term 'balance of payments' refers to:

  1. Systematic record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world in a given period (Current Account + Capital Account)
  2. Bank's income vs expenditure
  3. Government revenue vs expenditure (Budget)
  4. Export value vs import value (Trade Balance)

Answer: Systematic record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world in a given period (Current Account + Capital Account)

Explanation: Balance of Payments (BoP): complete record of all monetary transactions between a country and the rest of the world. Current Account: goods (trade balance), services, income, current transfers. Capital Account: FDI, FPI/FII, loans, banking capital. BoP always balances (accounting identity). BoP crisis: when foreign reserves are insufficient.

10. The term 'poverty line' in India is determined by:

  1. Minimum monthly per capita expenditure needed to meet basic food, clothing, and shelter needs — currently based on Tendulkar Committee methodology (modified by Rangarajan Committee)
  2. Annual income below Rs 1 lakh
  3. BPL card possession
  4. Urban/rural classification only

Answer: Minimum monthly per capita expenditure needed to meet basic food, clothing, and shelter needs — currently based on Tendulkar Committee methodology (modified by Rangarajan Committee)

Explanation: India's Poverty Line: Tendulkar Committee (2009): Rs 816/month urban, Rs 672/month rural (2011-12 prices). Rangarajan Committee (2014): higher threshold — Rs 1407 urban, Rs 972 rural. SECC (Socio-Economic and Caste Census) used for targeting welfare programs. About 21% of population below poverty line (World Bank $1.90/day PPP criterion).

11. Microfinance refers to:

  1. Provision of small loans (micro-credit), savings, insurance, and other financial services to low-income people who lack access to traditional banking
  2. Large loans to industry
  3. Export finance
  4. Home loans

Answer: Provision of small loans (micro-credit), savings, insurance, and other financial services to low-income people who lack access to traditional banking

Explanation: Microfinance: pioneered by Muhammad Yunus (Grameen Bank, Bangladesh, 1983 — Nobel Peace Prize 2006). In India: SHG-Bank Linkage Programme (RBI/NABARD, 1992) — women form self-help groups, save collectively, access bank credit. MFIs (Microfinance Institutions) like SKS/Bandhan. India has world's largest microfinance program.

12. What is 'opportunity cost' in economics?

  1. The value of the next best alternative foregone when making a choice — e.g., opportunity cost of studying = income from working
  2. The actual monetary cost of a good
  3. The cost of raw materials
  4. The labor cost of production

Answer: The value of the next best alternative foregone when making a choice — e.g., opportunity cost of studying = income from working

Explanation: Opportunity Cost: value of the best alternative foregone when a choice is made. Example: if you spend Rs 1 lakh on a business and it could have earned 8% in a bank (Rs 8,000), then opportunity cost = Rs 8,000/year. Fundamental to economics — every decision involves opportunity cost. Key to understanding rational decision-making.

13. India's Foreign Exchange Reserves are managed by:

  1. Reserve Bank of India (RBI) — held in gold, foreign currency assets (mainly USD T-bills), SDRs, and reserve tranche at IMF; ~$640 billion (2024)
  2. Finance Ministry
  3. SEBI
  4. EXIM Bank

Answer: Reserve Bank of India (RBI) — held in gold, foreign currency assets (mainly USD T-bills), SDRs, and reserve tranche at IMF; ~$640 billion (2024)

Explanation: RBI manages India's foreign exchange reserves: Foreign Currency Assets (FCA, largest component — USD, EUR, GBP, JPY treasury bills and bonds), Gold reserves (~800 tons), Special Drawing Rights (SDRs) from IMF, and Reserve Tranche Position at IMF. Used to: stabilize rupee, manage BoP, instill confidence. India: 4th largest forex reserves globally.

14. The Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana (PMMY) provides:

  1. Collateral-free loans up to Rs 10 lakh to non-corporate small businesses through 'Mudra loans' — Shishu (<Rs 50,000), Kishore (Rs 50,000-5 lakh), Tarun (Rs 5-10 lakh)
  2. Home loans
  3. Agricultural loans above Rs 1 crore
  4. Corporate loans

Answer: Collateral-free loans up to Rs 10 lakh to non-corporate small businesses through 'Mudra loans' — Shishu (<Rs 50,000), Kishore (Rs 50,000-5 lakh), Tarun (Rs 5-10 lakh)

Explanation: PMMY (Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency, 2015): provides Mudra loans to small/micro enterprises without collateral. Three categories: Shishu (up to Rs 50,000), Kishore (Rs 50,000-5 lakh), Tarun (Rs 5-10 lakh). Tarun Plus: up to Rs 20 lakh (2024). MUDRA Bank provides refinancing to MFIs, banks, NBFCs.

15. The Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana is meant for:

  1. Girl children — parents/guardians can open account for girls up to 10 years; high interest rate (~8%), tax-exempt, minimum Rs 250, maximum Rs 1.5 lakh/year; matures when girl turns 21
  2. Senior citizens
  3. BPL families
  4. Pregnant women

Answer: Girl children — parents/guardians can open account for girls up to 10 years; high interest rate (~8%), tax-exempt, minimum Rs 250, maximum Rs 1.5 lakh/year; matures when girl turns 21

Explanation: Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana (2015, 'Beti Bachao Beti Padhao' initiative): post office/bank savings scheme for girl child. Features: account opened before girl turns 10, deposits up to Rs 1.5 lakh/year, ~8% interest (one of highest for small savings), tax benefits under Section 80C, withdrawal for education (50%) at age 18, maturity at 21.

16. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) 2016 was enacted to:

  1. Provide time-bound resolution (180+90 days) of insolvency/bankruptcy cases for companies and individuals; replaced multiple earlier laws; improves India's ease of doing business rank
  2. Replace GST
  3. Regulate banks
  4. Control inflation

Answer: Provide time-bound resolution (180+90 days) of insolvency/bankruptcy cases for companies and individuals; replaced multiple earlier laws; improves India's ease of doing business rank

Explanation: IBC 2016: unified framework for resolving insolvency. Process: creditor/debtor files, Resolution Professional appointed, Committee of Creditors evaluates resolution plans within 180+90 days. Failing resolution → liquidation. Improved India's ranking in World Bank's Ease of Doing Business: Resolving Insolvency indicator improved dramatically.

17. The PM-KISAN (Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi) scheme provides:

  1. Rs 6,000 per year (Rs 2,000 per 4-month installment) directly to farmer families' bank accounts — covers all land-holding farmers; launched 2018
  2. Free seeds
  3. MSP guarantee
  4. Crop insurance free

Answer: Rs 6,000 per year (Rs 2,000 per 4-month installment) directly to farmer families' bank accounts — covers all land-holding farmers; launched 2018

Explanation: PM-KISAN (December 2018): income support of Rs 6,000/year in 3 installments of Rs 2,000 each to all farmer families (initially small/marginal, then extended to all landholding farmers). Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) to Aadhaar-linked bank accounts. About 9 crore+ beneficiaries. Annual expenditure: ~Rs 60,000 crore.

18. India's largest nationalized commercial bank is:

  1. State Bank of India (SBI) — formed 1955 (nationalized Imperial Bank); merged with 5 associate banks in 2017; largest bank by assets, branches (~22,500), and ATMs
  2. Punjab National Bank
  3. Bank of Baroda
  4. Canara Bank

Answer: State Bank of India (SBI) — formed 1955 (nationalized Imperial Bank); merged with 5 associate banks in 2017; largest bank by assets, branches (~22,500), and ATMs

Explanation: SBI (State Bank of India): India's largest public sector bank. Formed 1955 when Imperial Bank of India (1921) was nationalized. SBI merged with 5 associates (State Bank of Bikaner and Jaipur, Travancore, Mysore, Patiala, Hyderabad) and Bharatiya Mahila Bank in 2017. ~22,500 branches, 58,000+ ATMs, 50 crore+ customers.

19. The term 'quantitative easing' (QE) refers to:

  1. Central bank buys government securities or other financial assets from market to inject money into the economy — used when interest rates are near zero (unconventional monetary policy)
  2. Increasing bank reserve requirements
  3. Raising interest rates
  4. Reducing government spending

Answer: Central bank buys government securities or other financial assets from market to inject money into the economy — used when interest rates are near zero (unconventional monetary policy)

Explanation: QE: when conventional monetary policy (lowering interest rates) is insufficient (near-zero rates), central bank creates money and buys assets (government bonds, MBS) from banks. This: increases banks' reserves, lowers long-term interest rates, boosts credit, and stimulates economy. Used by US Fed, ECB, Bank of Japan after 2008 financial crisis.

20. The term 'moral hazard' in economics refers to:

  1. When an entity takes on more risk because it is protected from consequences (e.g., bank taking risky loans knowing government will bail it out; insured person being less careful)
  2. Unethical business practices
  3. Market manipulation
  4. Tax evasion

Answer: When an entity takes on more risk because it is protected from consequences (e.g., bank taking risky loans knowing government will bail it out; insured person being less careful)

Explanation: Moral hazard: tendency to take on more risk when protected from the consequences (risk is transferred to another party). Examples: bank risky lending (expecting government bailout — 'too big to fail'), insured driving recklessly, principal-agent problem (employee working less hard without monitoring). Key concept in insurance economics, banking regulation.

21. India's 'Ease of Doing Business' ranking has been assessed by:

  1. World Bank's Doing Business Report (discontinued 2021 after methodology controversy) — India improved from rank 142 (2014) to 63 (2020)
  2. WTO
  3. IMF
  4. UNDP

Answer: World Bank's Doing Business Report (discontinued 2021 after methodology controversy) — India improved from rank 142 (2014) to 63 (2020)

Explanation: World Bank's Doing Business Report (1990-2021): ranked countries on business regulations (starting business, getting credit, paying taxes, enforcing contracts, etc.). India rose from 142 (2014) to 63 (2020) — dramatic improvement. Report discontinued in 2021 due to data manipulation allegations. World Bank now developing new Business Ready (B-READY) report.

22. The Sovereign Gold Bond (SGB) scheme was launched to:

  1. Reduce physical demand for gold by offering government bonds denominated in gold grams (2.5% interest + capital gain linked to gold price); launched 2015 by RBI/MOF
  2. Nationalize gold reserves
  3. Import gold cheaply
  4. Sell government gold holdings

Answer: Reduce physical demand for gold by offering government bonds denominated in gold grams (2.5% interest + capital gain linked to gold price); launched 2015 by RBI/MOF

Explanation: Sovereign Gold Bonds (2015, RBI issues on behalf of Government): investors buy bonds denominated in gold grams (instead of physical gold). Benefits: 2.5% annual interest on invested amount, gold price appreciation, no storage cost, capital gains exempt on maturity. Reduces gold imports (India imports ~800-900 tons/year).

23. Pradhan Mantri Suraksha Bima Yojana (PMSBY) provides:

  1. Accidental death/disability insurance cover of Rs 2 lakh at Rs 20 premium per year — for bank account holders aged 18-70; launched 2015
  2. Health insurance
  3. Life insurance (non-accident)
  4. Pension

Answer: Accidental death/disability insurance cover of Rs 2 lakh at Rs 20 premium per year — for bank account holders aged 18-70; launched 2015

Explanation: PMSBY (2015): accidental death/permanent total disability: Rs 2 lakh; permanent partial disability: Rs 1 lakh. Annual premium: just Rs 20 (for 18-70 year olds with bank accounts). Auto-debit from bank account. Covers road accidents, drowning, accidental falls etc. Part of Jan Suraksha schemes. Pradhan Mantri Jeevan Jyoti Bima Yojana (PMJJBY): life insurance Rs 2 lakh, Rs 436/year.

24. The 'Dutch Auction' method used for government bond sales means:

  1. All winning bidders pay the lowest price (cut-off price) that clears the auction — price-competitive auction method used by RBI for T-bills and dated securities
  2. Everyone pays their own bid price (multiple price auction)
  3. Government fixes the price beforehand
  4. Only one bidder wins

Answer: All winning bidders pay the lowest price (cut-off price) that clears the auction — price-competitive auction method used by RBI for T-bills and dated securities

Explanation: Dutch Auction (uniform price auction): bids arranged highest to lowest; securities allocated from highest bidders down until total issue amount exhausted; all successful bidders pay the cut-off price (lowest accepted bid). Used by RBI for G-Secs. Alternative: French Auction (multiple price) — each bidder pays own bid price.

25. The term 'deflation' means:

  1. Sustained decrease in the general price level (negative inflation rate) — can lead to deflationary spiral: falling prices → less production → unemployment → less demand → further price fall
  2. Temporary price decrease
  3. Decrease in value of currency abroad
  4. Reduction in money supply without price effects

Answer: Sustained decrease in the general price level (negative inflation rate) — can lead to deflationary spiral: falling prices → less production → unemployment → less demand → further price fall

Explanation: Deflation: persistent fall in general price level. Causes: demand-side collapse, excess supply, tight monetary policy. Dangers: deflationary spiral (debt-deflation: real debt burden rises), reduced investment (prices expected to fall further), falling wages, rising unemployment. Japan's 'lost decade' (1990s-2000s) is example. Central banks try to avoid deflation.
